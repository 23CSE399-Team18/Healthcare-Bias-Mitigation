Healthcare Bias Mitigation:
a multi-method approach across diverse clinical applications

Three stages against bias: pre-processing repairs the data of four disease cohorts (eight biases, every method benchmarked on every metric, the best-ranked candidate method applied), the latent stage maps the four diseases and a cross-hospital ICU cohort into one shared feature space, and in-processing trains the model itself to be fair, calibrated and robust in hospitals it has never seen.

Background & context

Motivation

Biased, demographically skewed data

Healthcare AI systems are frequently developed using datasets that carry sampling bias and are demographically skewed, which restricts fairness and equitable performance across diverse patient populations.

Accuracy-only optimisation

Conventional machine learning models primarily optimize for overall accuracy, often neglecting performance disparities affecting underrepresented and vulnerable groups.

Unresolved challenges

Research Gaps

Gap 1 · Stage-isolated bias handling

Most bias mitigation techniques operate only at a single level (data or algorithm or output), and do not analyze how bias propagates across the entire ML pipeline.

Gap 2 · Lack of clinical risk awareness

Fairness metrics optimize statistical parity but ignore that some errors have medical consequences that are clinically more harmful than others.

Gap 3 · Absence of continuous fairness monitoring

Existing studies evaluate fairness offline on retrospective datasets and lack an integrated auditing mechanism that monitors demographic performance and detects fairness drift after deployment.

Gap 4 · Human–AI interaction neglect

Algorithmic fairness research rarely considers decision-level bias arising from clinician interpretation.

Core project goals

Objectives

Overall problem

Healthcare AI models trained on real-world clinical data often exhibit uneven predictive reliability across patient groups, resulting in unfair and unsafe clinical decision support. Existing solutions typically address these issues at isolated stages of the learning pipeline, limiting robustness for high-risk patient outcomes.

Objective 1

Design a healthcare AI framework incorporating equitable data collection and pre-processing techniques to reduce data-related bias and unfair clinical decision outcomes.

Objective 2

Implement in-processing bias mitigation strategies within the learning pipeline to improve fairness in healthcare AI models.

Objective 3

Apply post-processing bias mitigation techniques to reduce prediction disparities and improve fairness across patient demographics.

Objective 4

Incorporate clinical risk-aware learning and privacy-preserving fairness mechanisms to minimize high-risk misclassification errors and enable local (on-device) model operation without exposing patient data.

Formal problem definition

Problem Statement

Core problem

To develop a clinically risk-aware, multi-stage bias mitigation framework that ensures fairness and consistent predictive performance across diverse patient populations, reducing unfair and unsafe clinical decision support.

End-to-end workflow

Bias-aware pipeline

Every step is fitted on training rows; methods are scored on training and validation rows; the test split is only used for the final check.

1

Pre-processing

Repair the data of each disease.

  • 8 data biases, 53 methods
  • best-ranked candidate method applied, baselines kept as comparison
  • one dashboard per bias
2

Latent space

Put all cohorts into one shared 16-D feature space.

  • DGCCA, SAINT, MV-DAE vs PCA
  • chosen by CKA, Macro-F1, Silhouette, NMI/ARI
  • 4 diseases + cross-hospital ICU cohort
3

In-processing

Train the model to be fair, calibrated and robust.

  • 4 model biases, 16 methods, ERM = before
  • best mean rank over each bias's 4 metrics
  • final check on unseen hospitals + MIMIC

Latent feature extraction

One common latent space for four diseases and the cross-hospital ICU cohort

The diseases come from different surveys and hospitals with different variables, so each gets its own encoder; all encoders write into the same 16 coordinates. A good shared space keeps each outcome predictable (Macro-F1, Silhouette), makes matching patient groups line up across datasets (CKA) and stops the coordinates from simply revealing which dataset a patient came from (NMI/ARI). MIMIC-IV rows are only encoded, never trained on, so the in-processing cross-hospital check stays external.

Watch the cohorts merge

Each dot is one real validation record (300 per cohort). Before: every dataset is compressed on its own (PCA), so there is no shared meaning. Press a method to move the same records into its shared space; each view is only rotated and uniformly scaled to fit, so distances between records keep their proportions (the few most extreme records sit on the edge).

CKA ↑ and Macro-F1 ↑ are better when higher, NMI ↓ and ARI ↓ when lower (validation split).

Deep Generalized CCA (DGCCA)

Dataset-specific networks maximise the MAXVAR correlation of stratum-matched views (age × sex × BMI; age × sex for the ICU cohort, which has no BMI), followed by a global GCCA projection.

SAINT

Feature tokens with column attention and inter-sample attention to a fixed bank of training rows; contrastive and denoising pre-training.

Multi-View Denoising Autoencoder

Dataset-specific encoders/decoders with masking noise, a shared 16-D code and an MMD alignment term.

CKA ↑

Cross-dataset alignment of the latent features in the common space.

Macro-F1 ↑

Clinical information preserved for each disease's outcome.

Silhouette ↑

How clearly clinical classes separate in the latent space.

NMI / ARI ↓

Dataset-specific information left in the common space (lower = more domain-invariant).

In-processing

Training the model itself to be fair, calibrated and robust

Pre-processing repairs the data; in-processing changes how the model learns. Every method trains the same small network on the latent features with a different training objective. Plain training (ERM) is the “before”. For each bias the method with the best mean rank over that bias's four metrics on the validation split is selected (it must stay within 0.05 AUROC of ERM) and checked once on the untouched test split. The cross-hospital bias uses the cross-hospital ICU cohort with each disease's recorded label: trained on 141 eICU hospitals, selected on 34 others, checked on 31 unseen eICU hospitals and on MIMIC-IV, a different hospital system.

01

Demographic prediction dependence

Once the true outcome is known, the prediction should carry no information about sex or race.

Methods

FairCare (adversarial fair representation), DANN, GroupDRO, Fair-ERM

Metrics

CMI, PAD, WGER, EOV

02

Intersectional / subgroup unfairness

Small combined groups (sex × age × race) must not be left behind by a model that is fair only on average.

Methods

FOMO, intersectional debiasing, fairness-constrained optimization, GroupDRO (intersectional)

Metrics

HVI, DF-ε, MSFV, IWGR

03

Predictive miscalibration

A predicted 70% risk should mean that about 70% of such patients have the outcome, and confident predictions should be right more often.

Methods

AvUC, CRL, uncertainty-weighted gradient training, calibration-regularized training

Metrics

UCE, AURC, E-AURC, MMCE

04

Cross-hospital generalization failure

The model should work as well in every hospital, state or region as in the ones it saw most often.

Methods

Fishr, V-REx, EQRM, Fish

Metrics

IEGVD, ERV, UQER, IDGA
